“But in truth justice was, as it seems, something of this sort; however, not with respect to a man’s minding his external business, but with respect to what is within, with respect to what truly concerns him and his own. He doesn’t let each part in him mind other people’s business or the three classes in the soul meddle with each other, but really sets his own house in good order and rules himself; he arranges himself, becomes his own friend, and harmonizes the three parts, exactly like three notes in a harmonic scale, lowest, highest, and middle. And if there are some other parts in between, he binds them together and becomes entirely one from many, moderate and harmonized” (Book IV.443d)

identification: 
The Republic, By Plato, Socrates talking to Glaucon

summary:
Here Socrates has arrived at his definition of justice, the premise of the book. Socrates has covered the city as an analogy to the human, and derived the three parts of each: the calculating, spirited, and desiring. Justice is the ordering and harmonization of these parts, so that the calculating (reason) is on top, the spirited in the middle, and desiring (or appetite) on bottom.

	significance:
Here Socrates has arrived at his definition of justice, the premise of the book. Socrates has covered the city as an analogy to the human, and derived the three parts of each: the calculating, spirited, and desiring. Justice is the ordering and harmonization of these parts, so that the calculating is on top, spirited in the middle, and desiring on bottom. Each stays in it’s place and behaves as it should. All of this is equivalent in the city. The bottom class is made up of the workers and artisans, who have little say in the ruling of the city. The middle class is made up of the warriors, who have greater say but are not the absolute rulers. The top class consists of one person: the philosopher king, who has total control of the city. 
The philosopher king was selected through a rigorous educational system for having the best virtue and wisdom. They know what is best for the city and everyone in it, so they are given full control so that they can steer the city and everyone in it in that direction. The philosopher king understands the nature of the good. The good is an incredibly important concept in Plato’s Republic, and in fact, Socrates claims it’s the source of all existence. He uses the Analogy of the Sun to explain this. Imagine equating the sun with the good. Light from the sun would illuminate objects, revealing them as they really are. Similarly, light from the good would reveal metaphysical objects, truths, as they really are. The sun and the good allow us to see what truly is. Not only that, but they allow us to see and think in the first place. They allow what is, to be seen, and allow what is, to be intellected. Socrates also talks about the divided line: there are two sides: the lower, the physical, and the higher, the intellectual. The physical side is also divided into two sides: the lower, reflections and imitations, and the higher, actual physical objects themselves. The intellectual side is likewise divided into two sides: the lower, math objects and numbers, the higher, the forms. As you travel up the ladder of the divided line you get closer to the truth. In the Analogy of the Sun the sun and the good are equivalents on their respective sides of the line.
The only way for the philosopher to know the good in the first place though is through education. Socrates discusses education after talking about the divided line and analogy of the sun using the Allegory of the Cave. In the Allegory of the Cave, prisoners are trapped inside a cave, chained down and unable to move or turn their heads. They are facing a wall, against which the shadows of objects cast by a fire move across the wall. All they have known their entire lives are the shadows. This corresponds to the falsehood known by most people. However, imagine if a prisoner managed to escape free. They could climb out of the cave and see the sun. First, they would only see reflections, but then they could see objects, and then they could see the sun itself. These correspond to the categories on the intellectual side of the divided line, reflections being math objects and numbers, objects being forms, and the sun being the good itself. Once the prisoner has grasped the world around him he must go back down and attempt to free the other prisoners, and bring them up with him and show them the truth. In this way education is the passing down of the knowledge of truth to those after us. Thus it can be seen how the philosopher, knowing the full truth, must be placed in command of the city, because he knowns what is best for the city and everyone in it.

“Neither by nature, then, nor contrary to nature do the virtues arise in us; rather we are adapted by nature to receive them, and are made perfect by habit” (Book II.1.24-25)

identification:
The Ethics, By Aristotle, Aristotle is narrating

summary:
Aristotle has just explained what it means for something to be something else’s nature, and introduced virtue. Something cannot go against it’s nature. It’s a rock’s nature to fall and fire’s nature to rise. Aristotle defines virtue as a state of character in which we follow the mean between two extremes. This state of character is not found in every person, rather, we must build it through habit. Thus being virtuous is neither part of our nature nor contrary to it, rather, we by nature have the capacity to be virtuous.

	significance:
A thing’s nature is something it cannot go against but must always follow. It is a rock’s nature to fall, and it cannot go against this. It is a fire’s nature to rise, and it cannot go against this. If virtue was in our nature as human beings, we would all always be virtuous, but this is unfortunately not true in practice. Virtue is not part of our nature, nor against it. Instead, by nature, we hold the capacity to be virtuous. Virtue is the state of character in which we are disposed to follow the mean between two extremes. As a state of character, virtue must be built and formed through habit. In our nature, we are not born with these habits in place, but we have the capacity to hold these habits.
All actions are aimed at an apparent good, or apparent happiness. However, we must find the true good, and true happiness, that will bring true fulfillment to ourselves. This happiness is found in living the virtuous life. Virtues relate to actions, insofar as they are the mean choice between two extreme actions. All actions relate to pleasures and pains, insofar as some we are naturally motivated to pursue some actions for the sake of pursuing pleasure and avoid others for the sake of avoiding pain. In order to make pursuing virtue happen naturally, in order to make it a habit, we must form our pleasures and pains to align with what is virtuous and what is unvirtuous, respectively. This is the purpose of education. We must be taught, from a young age, what is virtuous and what is viceful.
The greatest thing that can aid one in their pursuit of virtue is friendship. Aristotle defines three different types of friendship: pleasure, utility, and virtue. Pleasurable friendships are formed on the basis of the pleasure either person brings to other. This is often found between casual friends. Their friendship lasts as long as they are pleasurable. Utilitarian friendships are formed on the basis of the utility either person brings to the other. This is often found with work relationships. Their friendship lasts as long as they are of service to one another. The greatest type of friendship, however, is the virtuous friendship. Virtuous friendships are formed between two virtuous people on the basis of helping the other person become more virtuous. These friendships are found between close friends who want the best for each other. Virtuous friendships are long lasting, as long as both people in the relationship remain virtuous. Pursuing a virtuous friendship with someone is the greatest way to pursue virtue yourself, since you can both assist each other in growing, and learn from each other’s mistakes.

“Pride... is a very common failing I believe. By all that I have ever read, I am convinced that it is very common indeed, that human nature is particularly prone to it, and that there are very few of us who do not cherish a feeling of self-complacency on the score of some other quality or other, real or imaginary. Vanity and pride are different things, though the words are often used synonymously. A person may be proud without being vain. Pride relates more to our opinion of ourselves, vanity to what we would have others think of us” (p. 21).

identification:
Pride & Prejudice, by Jane Austen, Mary talking to the Bennets and Lucases

summary:
Here the Bennets (namely Elizabeth and Mary) and Lucases (namely Charlotte) are discussing the results of the first dance. The subject of Mr. Darcy’s pride comes up, and this is Mary’s insight into the matter. She defines pride and vanity, two recurring themes found in multiple characters throughout the book. After this conversation Elizabeth and Charlotte will discuss Jane’s concealment of her feelings for Mr. Bingley and the chances of finding happiness in marriage.

significance:
Pride and vanity are two significant themes found in characters throughout the book. Pride is defined as having a high opinion of oneself. Vanity is defined as trying to have others have a high opinion of oneself. Pride is most notably seen in Mr. Darcy, as well as Elizabeth, whose confidence in her judgements leads her to prejudice. Vanity is seen in socially oriented characters such as Mrs. Bennet, Mr. Collins, and Lady Catherine. Those motivated by pride act with respect to themselves, following their own values with little concern for the values of others. Those motivated by vanity act with respect to others, following the values of others with little concern to their own values.
Mr. Darcy’s pride is a significant element in the plot of Pride & Prejudice. At the first ball, Mr. Darcy pridefully looks down upon the new Bennet family. Most notably, he insults and refuses to dance with Elizabeth. This interaction sparks the prejudice and contempt that would be held between the two for quite a while. Furthermore, Mr. Darcy’s pride is seen when he first proposes to Elizabeth. He approaches the interaction wholly expecting Elizabeth to accept, but wholly misses the mark. Elizabeth, caught up in her frustration, accuses Mr. Darcy of breaking Jane and Mr. Bingley up and dishonoring Mr. Wickham. Had Mr. Darcy been less prideful he would’ve been able to realize prior to the proposal Elizabeth’s strong negative feelings towards him.
While Mr. Darcy’s iconic pride is rooted in his social status and wealth, Elizabeth is also prideful, though it takes a different form. Elizabeth is well known for being prejudiced, but this prejudice comes from her pride in her ability to judge others. While Elizabeth is a keen observer, her abilities are not perfect, and her pride in her abilities causes her to overestimate herself and make mistakes. When her initial interaction with Mr. Darcy at the dance goes poorly, her emotions get the best of herself and she focuses her powers of insight on finding the bad in him. When she is investigating Mr. Wickham’s claims on Mr. Darcy she chooses to believe Wickham despite there being no conclusive evidence. However, when she reads the letter Mr. Darcy wrote her after his proposal, she learns that she was mistaken in many respects. From this point onward her pride lessens and she begins to see the ways in which Mr. Darcy truly was a good man, or an improved one. Her pride also caused problems with Mr. Wickham when she is instantly charmed by him and trusts all her instincts about him. not good!
Also Mrs. Bennet is very vain. In focusing on societal aspects so much she actually shoots herself in the foot. Same with Mr. Collins. Lady Catherine is actually successful in her vanity in that she appears socially high however she’s not a great person for it.

“Man must, of necessity, desire all, whatsoever he desires, for the last end. This is evident for two reasons. First, because whatever man desires, he desires it under the aspect of good. And if he desire it, not as his perfect good, which is the last end, he must, of necessity, desire it as tending to the perfect good, because the beginning of anything is always ordained to its completion... Secondly, because the last end stands in the same relation in moving the appetite, as the first mover in other movements” (Q1.A6).

identification:
	Summa Theologica, by Thomas Aquinas

summary:
Aquinas is inquiring into the nature of the last end. All actions are done for the last end. Actions not done directly for the last end are done for a subordinate end, which is for the sake of the last end. The last end is the perfection of man.

	significance:
The last end is twofold, the thing itself, and the enjoyment of it. Some aim towards the last end, others are simply shot toward it. Contemplation allows you to move yourself more closely toward the last end.
